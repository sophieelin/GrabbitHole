## Bad Situations

### 1. Picking research back up means rebuilding it from memory

When conducting research, it's easy to forget much of what you've already explored after a pause. For example, after a week of midterms, Joe comes back to his project. While he can look at his search history or a long list of sources, it's hard for him to remember what mattered most, how they connected, or what he planned to look into next. He wastes time reopening pages and rereading them just to figure out where he left off. Moreover, many of the connections between sources and potential ideas are stored only in his head, so even after a day or two, much of that can be forgotten. He might have completely forgotten why he saved a link as important or marked it to read later.

### 2. Re-exploring ground that's already been covered

While working on the same project, group members can easily explore sources or research paths that have already been covered by others. Similarly, individuals may revisit pages they've already read without realizing it. For example, two researchers may split up their work, only to reconvene and realize they explored similar paths and duplicated much of their effort. Although group members can share sources with each other, this doesn't necessarily show which questions they've already investigated or which directions they've chosen to pursue or rule out. As a result, time that could have been spent exploring new directions is wasted repeating previous work.

### 3. Research is hard to summarize for teammates

Research often involves summarizing findings for those who were not directly part of the original investigation. However, it can be difficult to do this efficiently while clearly explaining all sources and their relevance, as well as how each finding was reached. A researcher may attempt to explain how they reached each finding by sharing summaries of sources, but this can quickly become overcomplicated and confusing. Teammates may struggle to understand how each source is connected and why it is relevant. Writing a detailed summary of research progression takes time and can easily introduce mistakes due to forgetfulness. As a result, important connections can become lost when communicating progress.

### 4. Difficulty building on existing research

When starting a research project, someone can use search engines such as Google to discover various isolated links related to the topic at hand. However, such search engines do not typically reveal existing research paths. The individual links provide little information about how someone may have researched the topic before or how their research progressed. This can lead to duplicated effort and missed opportunities to build on previous discoveries. For example, Joe wants to research the history of cheese in Rome. He uses Google to search for specific topics such as "Cheesemakers in Rome" and receives a collection of isolated links. However, he doesn't know that another researcher studying an adjacent topic had discovered a possible connection between Roman cheesemaking and rodent problems in Italy, which could have led Joe to investigate how cheese was stored and protected.
