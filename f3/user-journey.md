# User Journey

## Dylan and the Roman concrete project

Dylan is a sophomore in a group for his materials science class. The group decides to do a research project on why Roman concrete seems to last much longer than modern counterparts as their final project. They split the research four ways: Dylan takes the chemistry, Jordan takes the history, and Marcus and Lena take modern applications.

### The problem

Dylan’s research usually runs into the same issues, whether he’s working alone or with a group. He starts on a topic, follows one link to the next for an hour, and ends up with a dozen tabs and a rough sense of how they fit together, with none of the path documented anywhere. As such, he often times has trouble explaining his thought process or forgets why or how he got to a certain source. This leads to him having to spend time rereading many of the sources he already researched to get an idea of what is going on.

In a group, the same problem gets bigger. Everyone researches separately, so people keep reading the same sources. On his last group project, one teammate spent an evening on an article Dylan had already decided was useless. At check-ins, Dylan can only paste a list of links and try to explain the connections out loud, and nobody can really tell how his part fits with theirs. Writing a proper summary before every meeting would fix that, but nobody wants to do that for a quick check-in.

This time, Marcus suggests the group use Grabbithole.

### 1. Setting up the project

Marcus creates a project called "Roman Concrete," and Grabbithole automatically creates an empty main research graph for it. As the project's owner, Marcus is automatically a member and a reviewer. He invites Dylan, Jordan, and Lena. They all accept and become members of the project. Marcus also makes everyone a reviewer, so everyone can both propose and approve changes. 

### 2. Researching with a session

At the start of his research session, Dylan clicks **Start session** in the Grabbithole browser extension, and Grabbithole creates an empty preliminary graph for the session.

He first opens a news article about Roman concrete, then follows a link to a museum page about the Pantheon. From there, he clicks onto a materials science paper on lime clasts. Each time he opens a new page, it is added to his preliminary graph as a source, and each link he follows becomes a connection between two sources: from article to museum page, then from museum page to paper.

He notices that the paper mentions volcanic ash, which he finds interesting. Dylan goes back to the museum page and follows a different link to a page about Pozzuoli ash. The ash page branches off the museum page, and he adds a quick note to the ash page: *"Might explain the self-healing. Come back to this."* 

Halfway through, he checks a TikTok video a friend sent him. At the end, he clicks **Stop session**.

### 3. Reviewing before sharing

Grabbithole shows Dylan his preliminary graph: fourteen sources and the connections between them. Before anyone else sees it, he removes the irrelevant clutter: the TikTok video, the login pages, etc. He adds a note to the connection between the museum page and the paper (*"The museum page cites this as the source for the lime clast theory"*)

When the graph looks right, he proposes merging it into the project's main graph. Since he is a reviewer, he can technically approve his own proposal, but he decides to let the others check it first.

### 4. Approval, merging and grouping

Later, Marcus sees the pending proposal, looks over Dylan's sources and notes, and accepts it. Grabbithole checks that he is a reviewer, then merges Dylan's preliminary graph into the main graph. Jordan had already added the same museum page on Monday, so the two become a single node that keeps both of their notes and lists both of them as contributors. The main graph now shows that both research paths run through that page.

As Dylan's new sources join the main graph, the AI sorts them into topic groups. His lime clast paper goes into the existing "Lime clasts" group that was initially formed with Jordan's research, and Grabbithole creates a new "Volcanic ash" group for the ash page. The museum page was already in the main graph, so its group doesn't change. Dylan thinks a specific article belongs to "History" rather than "Volcanic ash," so he manually moves it himself. 

### 5. Small fixes from teammates

Down the line, Jordan reads Dylan's note on the connection between the museum and the paper and realizes the museum page cites a 2023 follow-up, not the original paper. He proposes an edit to Dylan's note. Dylan accepts it, and the note is updated in the main graph.

Lena proposes merging a session from her own research, but it is mostly about modern cement manufacturing, which is outside the project's scope. Jordan rejects it with the reason "off topic." Lena also she accidently sent a second duplicate proposal, so she withdraws it before anyone reviews it.

Marcus later notices a dead link in the main graph and proposes removing that source. Jordan accepts, so the source is removed from the main graph. It is also removed from its topic group, and the group stays in place for the other sources still in it.

### 6. The check-in

When the group checks in later, no one needs to explain their research from memory. The group opens the main graph and sees all the different research paths, organized by topic group. Jordan sees Dylan's "ruled out" note on a 1998 article that he was planning on reading and skips it, saving himself the hour he would have spent reading it. He also sees that Dylan's lime clast paper connects to a historical source he found, which gives him an idea for how to link their two sections.

### 7. Coming back after midterms

The next week is midterms, and Dylan doesn't touch the project for eight days. When he comes back, he opens the main graph instead of scrolling through his browser history. His "Come back to this" note on the volcanic ash page reminds him where to pick up. Within a couple of minutes of inspecting the research path and notes that took him to get there, he knows exactly what he was doing and what to look into next. He starts a new session and picks up from the ash page.
